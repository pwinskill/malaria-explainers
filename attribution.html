Attributing impact of malaria interventions

Malaria interventions are rarely deployed on their own. A new tool is usually added to a setting that already has others in place. Because the interventions act on a shared and non-linear pathway to clinical cases, their effects do not simply add up. The impact credited to any one intervention, and so its apparent cost-effectiveness, then depends on the order in which the interventions' impacts are considered.

1 Combined intervention impact does not add up

Evidence

When two or more malaria interventions are used together, the reduction in cases they achieve jointly is smaller than the sum of the reductions each achieves on its own. A case prevented by one tool cannot be prevented again by another, and each tool acts on the cases that remain after the others. Modelled evaluations of intervention combinations show this diminishing return: adding a further tool to an already well-controlled setting averts fewer cases than the same tool would avert on its own.2

Cost-effectiveness is usually assessed at the margin: an intervention is credited with the cases it averts over and above what is already in place. This incremental approach is standard and, for the decision it is built for, appropriate.3 But because combined impact saturates, the incremental impact of a given intervention is not a fixed property of that intervention. It depends on the backdrop it is measured against.

Mechanism

Impact is not linear with effort. If each unit of control averted the same number of cases, the order of counting would not matter. But because the interventions overlap, impact saturates: an intervention assessed first acts against the full burden and is credited with greater impact than if it were assessed later, acting against a shrinking remainder of the burden. The interventions together avert the same total whichever way they are counted; what changes is how much of that total is assigned to each.

This direction holds when the interventions overlap multiplicatively, each acting on a shared pool of remaining cases. It is not universal. Where two or more interventions reduce transmission itself, successive reductions move the setting along the saturating curve from transmission intensity to clinical cases (the subject of the non-linearities explainer). At high baseline transmission the setting starts on the plateau of that curve, where the first reduction averts little and a later one, acting on a steeper part, can avert more: combined impact then exceeds the sum of the separate impacts, and the intervention counted first is credited with less, not more. The toy model below has a single transmission-reducing intervention, so it does not encounter this reversal.

2 Why this matters for comparing cost-effectiveness

Evidence

A new intervention is almost always evaluated as an addition to the existing package, so it is credited with only the cases it averts on top of that package. Where control is already good, that residual is small, and the cost per case averted of a genuinely effective new tool can look high. The same tool assessed against an untreated setting, as a first intervention, would look far more cost-effective. Neither figure is wrong; they answer different questions.

Mechanism

Two consequences follow. First, a newer intervention is systematically credited against a fuller backdrop than an older one, so a like-for-like ranking of their cost per case averted can favour whichever was assessed first. In the toy, the same control is credited with far fewer cases when introduced last than when assessed on its own, so at equal cost it looks much less cost-effective on top of the others. Second, cost-effectiveness estimates carried across settings or studies are only comparable when the backdrop is the same. The backdrop is part of the estimate, not a detail outside it.

Not only interventions: funders too

The same arithmetic applies wherever a shared impact is split, not only across tools. Where funding is fragmented across separate funders or programmes, each paying for part of the same control effort, attributing impact to any one funder meets the identical problem: a funder's credited impact depends on what the others fund and on the order the contributions are counted, so the same contribution can look highly cost-effective assessed on its own and marginal assessed on top of the rest. A fixed joint impact cannot be divided cleanly between siloed funders; the same choices, and caveats, apply as for individual interventions.

Ways this is handled

There is no single correct way to divide credit between combined interventions; the appropriate choice depends on the question being asked. Some of the approaches in use:

  • Incremental against current practice. Credit an intervention with what it averts on top of the tools already in place. This answers the operational question "should this be added here, now?" directly, and is the basis of standard cost-effectiveness analysis.3 Its estimates are specific to that backdrop and are not a fixed property of the intervention.
  • Against a common baseline. Assess each candidate intervention against the same defined starting point (for example, no intervention, or a fixed core package). This makes cost per case averted comparable across candidates, at the cost of not describing the actual decision, which is usually about adding to whatever is already there.
  • Reporting the backdrop. Keep the incremental estimate but state the assumed package explicitly, so figures are not compared across mismatched backdrops. This adds no method, only the discipline of not stripping the estimate of its context.
  • Averaging over orders. Credit each intervention with its added impact averaged over every order in which the tools could be introduced. This splits the fixed combined total symmetrically and does not privilege whichever tool was counted first; in cooperative game theory the same average is the Shapley value.4 It suits dividing credit across a portfolio rather than deciding a single addition, and the number of orders grows quickly with the number of interventions.
  • Evaluating the package. Compare whole packages of interventions against each other, rather than crediting individual components, when the decision is about which combination to fund rather than which single tool to add.2

What to notice

The total is fixed; only the split moves. Change the order control and the combined cases averted at the top of the staircase does not change. What changes is how that fixed total is divided between the three controls. Crediting one more always credits the others less.

Going first is worth more than going last. Each control is credited with more the earlier it is introduced, because it is then measured against a fuller burden; introduced later, it is measured only against what the others have already removed. The same control can be credited several times more when it goes first than when it goes last. Which control has the largest step also depends on how strong each is, not on position alone: push the transmission slider to its highest, where reducing transmission averts little, and a direct-protection control added later can be credited more than A added first.

Equal cost, unequal cost-effectiveness. All three interventions cost the same, so their cost per case averted is set entirely by the cases they are credited with. The same control assessed later is credited with fewer cases, so its cost per case averted rises, though nothing about it has changed.

Cost-effectiveness comparisons need a shared backdrop. Because the backdrop sets the incremental impact, cost per case averted is only comparable between interventions measured against the same starting point. A ranking that mixes backdrops can mislead.

Methods. A deliberately simple, illustrative toy model showing the shape of how attributed impact and cost-effectiveness depend on the order in which combined interventions are counted; not a specific setting, and not to be used for decision making. The intervention effects and costs are fixed, arbitrary, illustrative choices.

  • Burden curve. Clinical cases per person-year are a function f(EIR) of transmission intensity, taken from the equilibrium solution of the Griffin model1 as ported in the age-distribution explainer. Over the range used here f rises and saturates monotonically with EIR (all-age clinical incidence), so it provides a clean, non-linear map from transmission to cases. The transmission slider sets EIR on a log scale; parasite prevalence is shown as a more familiar index.
  • The three interventions. A reduces transmission by a fixed fraction (so with A in place the setting sits lower on the curve above) and also gives direct protection (a fixed multiplicative reduction of the remaining clinical cases). B and C give direct protection only. The fixed illustrative values are: A reduces transmission by 50% and clinical cases by a further 30%; B reduces clinical cases by 50% and C by 40%. Each tool is taken to cover the whole population, with its direct effect reducing all-age clinical incidence uniformly: a deliberate simplification, since real child-targeted tools (such as chemoprevention or a vaccine) would differ. A's transmission and direct effects are an arbitrary split of a single underlying mechanism.
  • Cases and the set function. For a set S of deployed controls, cases = N·f(EIRS)·∏(1 − dj), where EIRS is the reduced EIR if A is in S and the baseline EIR otherwise, and the product runs over the direct-protection effects of the tools in S (A's own direct effect included when A is present). Cases averted is baseline cases minus this. N = 100,000 people. Because every control multiplies the shared pool of cases, the combination is sub-additive.
  • Why the order effect here does not depend on the burden curve. Because only A acts on transmission, f enters through a single factor and the cases function reduces exactly to N·f(EIR0)·∏mi, where mA = ρ(1 − dA) with ρ = f(EIRA)/f(EIR0), and mB, mC are the direct effects. Every increment then shrinks as further controls are added (the set function is submodular), and A is credited in first position with exactly 1/(mBmC) = 3.3 times its last-position credit, at every transmission level. The shape of f sets how strong A is, through ρ, but contributes nothing to the ordering. With two or more transmission-reducing controls that separation breaks, because successive reductions move along f in stages: at high baseline transmission, on the plateau of f, a later reduction can avert more than an earlier one, so neither sub-additivity nor the first-is-worth-more ordering is guaranteed.
  • Attribution. For a chosen order, each control is credited with the cases averted when it is added to the ones before it (the staircase steps). These increments sum to the same combined total for every order. The fair share credits each control with its added impact averaged over all six orders; it also sums to the combined total, and equals the Shapley value4 of the cases-averted set function.
  • Cost-effectiveness. The three interventions are given the same cost (an illustrative $1 per person per year). Cost per case averted for a control is that cost divided by the cases credited to it in the chosen order. Because the costs are equal, the comparison depends only on the credited impact, and does not depend on the population size.
  • Alignment with the R cross-check. The cross-check in validation/validate_attribution.R computes f(EIR) from malariaEquilibrium on the same grid and confirms that the combined total is identical for all six orders, that each order's increments sum to that total, that the fair-share values sum to it as well, and that A's first-position credit is 1/((1 − dB)(1 − dC)) times its last-position credit at every transmission level.
