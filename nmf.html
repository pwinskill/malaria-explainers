Non-malaria fevers and inflated malaria case counts

Routine surveillance records a malaria case when a person with a fever tests positive. But where malaria is endemic, many infections are asymptomatic, and a fever from another cause can coincide with one. Those fevers test positive and are recorded as malaria, even though malaria did not cause them. Because non-malaria fevers occur year-round while true malaria is seasonal, this over-counting is not uniform: it distorts the seasonal shape of the data.1

1 Routine data counts test-positive fevers, not clinical malaria

Evidence

Following the shift to test-based case management, a person presenting at a health facility with a fever is given a diagnostic test, usually a rapid diagnostic test (RDT), before antimalarials are prescribed.1 Routine surveillance then records a confirmed malaria case for each febrile individual who tests positive. The recorded count is therefore a count of test-positive fevers presenting for care, not a direct count of illness caused by malaria.

Mechanism

A positive test establishes that the person carries a detectable infection. It does not establish that the infection is the cause of their fever. Two conditions have to hold for a recorded case to be a true clinical malaria case: the person must be carrying parasites, and those parasites must be the reason they are febrile. Where the two are not causally linked, the case is recorded all the same. The routine count is thus the sum of true clinical malaria and a second, coincidental component.

2 Where malaria is endemic, much infection is asymptomatic

Evidence

In endemic settings a large share of the population carries a bloodstream infection at any given time without being ill from it.2 Acquired immunity allows people to tolerate parasites, and untreated infections can persist for weeks to months, so this asymptomatic reservoir is both substantial and slow to turn over. The higher the transmission intensity, the larger the share of people carrying a detectable infection.

Mechanism

Because carriage builds up over the preceding weeks of transmission and clears only slowly, the prevalence of detectable infection is a broadened, lagged and floored version of the transmission signal: it rises after the season is under way, falls gradually once transmission recedes, and does not collapse in the low season the way new clinical illness does. Anyone tested during the low season still has an appreciable chance of a positive result, whether or not malaria is making them ill.

3 Constant non-malaria fevers meet a seasonal reservoir

Evidence

Fevers have many causes other than malaria — respiratory and other infections chief among them — and across a year these are far more common than malaria-caused fevers.1 Their rate is higher in young children, and it does not track the malaria season closely; to a first approximation it can be treated as roughly constant through the year, and here it is held constant to isolate its effect.

Mechanism

A person with a non-malaria fever who is tested returns a positive result whenever they happen to be carrying a detectable infection. The number of such coincidental positives is the non-malaria fever rate multiplied by the prevalence of detectable infection. Holding the fever rate constant, this coincidental count follows the asymptomatic reservoir through the year. Set against a true clinical incidence that collapses in the low season, a component that only follows the slowly-varying reservoir comes to dominate the recorded count there — so the over-counting is largest, in relative terms, exactly where and when true malaria is lowest.

The recorded line sits above the truth all year; the gap is the coincidental positives. In the peak the gap is small relative to a large true signal, but in the low season the true signal has collapsed while the reservoir persists, so the same non-malaria fevers make up most of what is recorded. Raising the fever rate lifts the whole recorded curve and flattens its apparent seasonality.

What to notice

The recorded count is always at or above the truth. Every non-malaria fever that coincides with a detectable infection adds a case that malaria did not cause. There is no setting of the sliders where routine data undercounts in this model; the whole effect is inflation. (A real system also misses true cases that never present or test negative, which this toy leaves aside.)

The distortion is differential across the season. Compare the two over-count readouts. In the peak season, recorded cases run only modestly above the truth, because they are measured against a large true signal; in the low season they can be several times the truth, because true clinical malaria has fallen to its floor while the asymptomatic reservoir — and the constant non-malaria fever rate that samples it — has not. The apparent season looks flatter and longer than the real one.

The more concentrated the season, the worse the low-season distortion. Raise the seasonality slider so the true peak is narrow and its off-season floor is low. The low-season over-count climbs, because the true signal it is measured against has almost vanished while the coincidental component holds up.

Higher transmission enlarges the reservoir, not just the disease. Raise the transmission intensity: the asymptomatic prevalence rises, so a fixed non-malaria fever rate yields more coincidental positives and a larger over-count, even though the true-incidence curve here is drawn at a fixed relative scale. Where infection is common, a positive test carries less information about the cause of a fever.2

Reading routine data as clinical burden overstates malaria. The share of recorded cases not caused by malaria is substantial at plausible fever rates, and largest in low-transmission periods and places.3 Trends and seasonal profiles built from confirmed-case counts inherit this bias, most heavily off-peak.

Methods. A deliberately simple, illustrative toy model showing the shape of how non-malaria fevers inflate routine malaria counts, not a specific setting; not to be used for decision making. Cases are shown in relative units on an arbitrary scale, so only the shape and the relative over-counts are meaningful. It models a single focal group (young children).

  • True clinical malaria. True clinical incidence follows a periodic curve C(t) = f + (1 − f)·s(t), where s(t) = ((1 + cos(φ(t)))/2)p is a single yearly peak fixed at 1. The seasonality slider raises the peakiness p = 1 + 8S + 16S4 and lowers the off-season floor f = (1 − S)1.5 together; the speed of take-off skews the phase φ so the rise into the peak spans a fraction r of the year (r = 0.67, 0.5, 0.33 for slow, medium, fast), with the peak held at tpeak. This is the same seasonal case curve used in the seasonality explainer.
  • Asymptomatic detectable prevalence. The prevalence of detectable infection A(t) is the periodic steady state of a susceptible–infected–susceptible relation dA/dt = β·C(t)·(1 − A) − A/D, driven by the seasonal transmission signal C(t) with mean infection duration D = 80 days, integrated around the year to convergence. The clearance term and finite duration make A a broadened, lagged, floored version of C. The transmission intensity slider sets β = 0.003·exp(E/33), so a higher setting raises the reservoir and its off-season floor.
  • Non-malaria fevers and recorded cases. The non-malaria fever rate N is held constant through the year and expressed as episodes per child per year, with a normalised value n = N/6 relative to a reference of 6. The falsely-attributed count is the fever rate times the chance of a coincident detectable infection, κ·n·A(t) with κ = 1 a fixed relative scale. Recorded cases are R(t) = C(t) + κ·n·A(t); the shaded band is the second term. The model assumes constant treatment-seeking and universal testing of fevers, so it isolates the coincidence effect rather than modelling access to care.
  • Readouts. The year is split at half the peak true incidence: peak-season days have C ≥ ½·max C, low-season days below it. The peak- and low-season over-counts are the recorded total divided by the true total over those days, ∫R / ∫C (the low-season figure is shown as "—" for a perennial setting with no distinct low season). The share of recorded cases not caused by malaria is the falsely-attributed total as a fraction of the recorded total, ∫(R − C) / ∫R, over the whole year.
  • What is left out. The true-incidence curve is drawn at a fixed relative scale, so the transmission-intensity slider changes only the reservoir, not the height of the true peak; in reality higher transmission raises both, while shifting the balance towards asymptomatic carriage. The toy also omits imperfect and age-varying treatment-seeking, test sensitivity and specificity, and the under-counting of true cases that never present — all of which a full accounting would include. It captures only the coincidence mechanism that inflates confirmed-case counts.
  • Relation to published estimates. The scale κ and the reservoir mapping are chosen so that at plausible settings the share of recorded cases not caused by malaria falls in the broad range reported by Dalrymple et al, who estimate that in 2014 only about 28% of malaria-positive fevers in under-fives were causally attributable to malaria (about 10% of all fevers), implying routine positive counts can overstate clinical malaria by a large margin that grows as transmission falls.3 The non-malaria fever process mirrors the structure being added to malariasimulation,4 where an age-specific non-malaria fever rate drives treatment-seeking and a positive test in someone with a detectable infection is recorded as a case. The cross-check in validation/validate_nmf.R reproduces these curves and readouts in R.
