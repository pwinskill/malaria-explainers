Non-malaria fevers and inflated malaria case counts

Routine surveillance records a malaria case when a person with a fever tests positive. But where malaria is endemic, many infections are asymptomatic, and a fever from another cause can coincide with one. Those fevers test positive and are recorded as malaria, even though malaria did not cause them. Because non-malaria fevers occur year-round while true malaria is seasonal, this over-counting is not uniform: it distorts the seasonal shape of the data.1

1 Routine data counts test-positive fevers, not clinical malaria

Evidence

Following the shift to test-based case management, a person presenting at a health facility with a fever is given a diagnostic test, usually a rapid diagnostic test (RDT), before antimalarials are prescribed.1 Routine surveillance then records a confirmed malaria case for each febrile individual who tests positive. The recorded count is therefore a count of test-positive fevers presenting for care, not a direct count of illness caused by malaria.

Mechanism

A positive test establishes that the person carries a detectable infection. It does not establish that the infection is the cause of their fever. Two conditions have to hold for a recorded case to be a true clinical malaria case: the person must be carrying parasites, and those parasites must be the reason they are febrile. Where the two are not causally linked, the case is recorded all the same. The routine count is thus the sum of true clinical malaria and a second, coincidental component.

2 Where malaria is endemic, much infection is asymptomatic

Evidence

In endemic settings a large share of the population carries a bloodstream infection at any given time without being ill from it.2 Acquired immunity allows people to tolerate parasites, and untreated infections can persist for weeks to months, so this asymptomatic reservoir is both substantial and slow to turn over. As transmission intensity rises, the prevalence of detectable infection climbs faster than the rate of clinical illness, because immunity increasingly limits disease without clearing infection.

Mechanism

Because carriage builds up over the preceding weeks of transmission and clears only slowly, the prevalence of detectable infection is a broadened, lagged and floored version of the transmission signal: it rises after the season is under way, falls gradually once transmission recedes, and does not collapse in the low season the way new clinical illness does. Anyone tested during the low season still has an appreciable chance of a positive result, whether or not malaria is making them ill.

3 Constant non-malaria fevers meet a seasonal reservoir

Evidence

Fevers have many causes other than malaria — respiratory and other infections chief among them — and across a year these are far more common than malaria-caused fevers.1 Their rate is higher in young children, and it does not track the malaria season closely; to a first approximation it can be treated as roughly constant through the year, and here it is held constant to isolate its effect.

Mechanism

A person with a non-malaria fever who seeks care and is tested returns a positive result whenever they happen to be carrying a detectable infection. The number of such coincidental positives is the non-malaria fever rate times the share who are tested times the prevalence of detectable infection. Holding the fever rate constant, this coincidental count follows the asymptomatic reservoir through the year. Set against a true clinical incidence that collapses in the low season, a component that only follows the slowly-varying reservoir comes to dominate the recorded count there — so the over-counting is largest, in relative terms, exactly where and when true malaria is lowest.

The recorded line sits above the truth all year; the gap is the coincidental positives. In the peak the gap is small relative to a large true signal, but in the low season the true signal has collapsed while the reservoir persists, so the same non-malaria fevers make up most of what is recorded. Raising the fever rate lifts the whole recorded curve and flattens its apparent seasonality; raising treatment seeking scales both curves together.

What to notice

The recorded count is always at or above the truth. Every non-malaria fever that coincides with a detectable infection adds a case that malaria did not cause. There is no setting of the sliders where routine data undercounts in this model; the whole effect is inflation. (A real system also misses true cases that never present or test negative, which this toy leaves aside.)

The distortion is differential across the season. Compare the two over-count readouts. In the peak season, recorded cases run only modestly above the truth, because they are measured against a large true signal; in the low season they can be several times the truth, because true clinical malaria has fallen to its floor while the asymptomatic reservoir — and the constant non-malaria fever rate that samples it — has not. The apparent season looks flatter and longer than the real one.

The more concentrated the season, the worse the low-season distortion. Raise the seasonality slider so the true peak is narrow and its off-season floor is low. The low-season over-count climbs, because the true signal it is measured against has almost vanished while the coincidental component holds up.

Higher transmission enlarges the reservoir faster than the disease. Raise the transmission intensity: the prevalence of infection climbs faster than the rate of clinical illness, as immunity limits disease without clearing infection. So a fixed non-malaria fever rate yields more coincidental positives per true case, and the over-count grows. Where infection is common, a positive test carries less information about the cause of a fever.2

Better access records more cases but does not fix the bias. Move treatment seeking: it multiplies both the true malaria cases and the coincidental ones, so the recorded volume rises or falls, but the over-count ratios and the share of recorded cases not caused by malaria do not move. The misattribution is a property of the case definition and the reservoir, not of how many people present for care.3

Reading routine data as clinical burden overstates malaria. The share of recorded cases not caused by malaria is substantial at plausible fever rates, and largest in low-transmission periods and places.3 Trends and seasonal profiles built from confirmed-case counts inherit this bias, most heavily off-peak.

Methods. A deliberately simple, illustrative toy model showing the shape of how non-malaria fevers inflate routine malaria counts, not a specific setting; not to be used for decision making. The vertical axis is shown as an illustrative count of cases per 1000 children per month, but its absolute level is not calibrated to any setting, so only the shape and the relative over-counts are meaningful. It models a single focal group (young children), with the case peak fixed at mid-year and a symmetric rise and fall.

  • Seasonal shape. A periodic curve g(t) = f + (1 − f)·((1 + cos(2π(t − tpeak)/365))/2)p gives a single yearly peak fixed at 1. The seasonality slider raises the peakiness p = 1 + 8S + 16S4 and lowers the off-season floor f = (1 − S)1.5 together, from a near-flat perennial year to a sharp two-to-three month peak. This is the same seasonal curve used in the seasonality explainer, here with the peak fixed at mid-year.
  • Transmission sets the levels. The transmission-intensity slider (u = T/100) fixes the peak level of clinical incidence, c(u) = u/(u + 0.15), and of prevalence, v(u) = 0.9·u/(u + 0.5). Both rise with transmission, but clinical incidence saturates sooner: the ratio v/c grows with T, a stand-in for immunity limiting disease while infection persists.3 Clinical incidence through the year is C(t) = c(u)·g(t).
  • Prevalence. The prevalence of detectable infection is P(t) = v(u)·ĝ(t), where ĝ is a broadened, lagged, floored version of the seasonal shape, obtained by relaxing towards g with a timescale D = 80 days (dĝ/dt = (g − ĝ)/D, integrated around the year and normalised to a peak of 1). The finite duration keeps prevalence up in the low season after clinical incidence has collapsed.
  • Recorded cases. Writing the treatment-seeking share τ and the non-malaria fever rate as N episodes per child per year with a normalised value n = N/6, the true malaria cases recorded are M(t) = τ·C(t) and the falsely-attributed cases are X(t) = τ·κ·n·P(t) with κ = 0.9 a fixed relative scale. The routine-recorded count is R(t) = M(t) + X(t); the shaded band is X. Because τ multiplies both terms, it scales the recorded volume but cancels from every over-count.
  • Readouts. The year is split at half the peak clinical incidence: peak-season days have C ≥ ½·max C, low-season days below it. The peak- and low-season over-counts are the recorded total divided by the true malaria total over those days, ∫R / ∫M (the low-season figure is shown as "—" for a perennial setting with no distinct low season). The share of recorded cases not caused by malaria is ∫X / ∫R over the whole year. All three are independent of the treatment-seeking share.
  • What is left out. The toy omits imperfect and age-varying treatment-seeking (here a single share), test sensitivity and specificity, the under-counting of true cases that never present, and any seasonality or age-dependence in the non-malaria fever rate. It captures only the coincidence mechanism that inflates confirmed-case counts.
  • Relation to published estimates. The scale κ and the level mappings are chosen so that at plausible settings the share of recorded cases not caused by malaria falls in the broad range reported by Dalrymple et al, who estimate that in 2014 only about 28% of malaria-positive fevers in under-fives were causally attributable to malaria (about 10% of all fevers), implying routine positive counts can overstate clinical malaria by a large margin that grows as transmission rises.3 The non-malaria fever process mirrors the structure being added to malariasimulation,4 where an age-specific non-malaria fever rate drives treatment-seeking and a positive test in someone with a detectable infection is recorded as a case. The cross-check in validation/validate_nmf.R reproduces these curves and readouts in R.
